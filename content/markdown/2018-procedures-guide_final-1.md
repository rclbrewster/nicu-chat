B. Standard Chest Tube Procedure 
 
1. Perform Universal Protocol, identifying correct patient, site, and procedure. 
 
2. Review chest radiograph to determine site of air/fluid accumulation. 
 
a. Air collects in the uppermost areas of the chest (goal anterior placement) 
 
i. Transillumination can be used as an alternative to an xray in an emergency situation (rapid 
deterioration).  With the lights in the room turned down, a strong light source is placed on the 
anterior chest wall above the nipple and in the axilla. The affected side will usually appear 
hyperlucent and will “light up” when compared to the unaffected side.  
 
b. Fluid collects in the most dependent areas of the chest (goal posterior placement)

a. ANTERIOR: Position infant supine with the affected side elevated 60-75 degrees using blanket rolls. 
Position the arm with the shoulder internally-rotated and crossing the head. Secure using soft 
restraints. 
 
b. POSTERIOR: Position infant supine elevating the affected side 15-30 degrees using blanket rolls. 
Position the arm over the head and secure using soft restraints. 
 
5. Use landmarks to identify intended site of CT insertion.  
 
a. Identify the 4

b. Identify the midaxillary line.

C. Pigtail Chest Tube Procedure 
 
The newest and possibly a less traumatic form of treatment of pneumothoraces is the modified pigtail 
catheter. The pigtail catheter, also known by other names including the Fuhrman catheter, is a coiled, single-
lumen, polyurethane catheter and ranges in size from 5 to 12 French. In the NICU at LPCH, our pigtail 
catheter is labeled as follows: Cook 8.5Fr/15cm Fuhrman Pleural/Pneumopericardial Drainage set 
(stocked in lower left corner of supply room). 
 
The pigtail catheter is placed using the modified Seldinger technique. This method allows the practitioner to 
enter the chest with a needle, place a guide wire, gently dilate the skin, and slide the pigtail into place.

III. Complications 
 
A. Infection. 
 
B. Trauma. Including lung laceration or perforation, perforation and hemorrhage from a major vessel 
(intercostal, axillary, pulmonary, or mammary), puncture of viscus within path of tube, residual scarring, 
permanent damage to breast tissue, or chylothorax. 
 
1. Avoid complications with careful attention to landmarks. 
 
2. Avoid use of the standard trocar to decrease bleeding risk. Bleeding may stop during suctioning. If 
bleeding persists, surgical consultation is needed. 
 
C. Nerve damage. 
 
D. Misplacement of tube. Tube outside pleural cavity or side hole outside pleural space. 
 
E. Subcutaneous emphysema. Leak of tension pneumothorax through pleural opening. 
 
F. Equipment malfunction. Blockage of tube, leak in evacuation system (usually at connection sites), 
inappropriate suction pressure.

Thoracentesis

I. Indications 
 
A. Temporary evacuation of life threatening air or fluid accumulation. 
 
B. Emergency evacuation of large fluid accumulation. 
 
II. Precautions/Contraindications

B. Relative clinical stability to allow time for thoracostomy tube placement.

Pre-medication for Endotracheal Intubation 
 
Janene H. Fuerch, M.D. 
 
I. Background 
 
A. Endotracheal intubation in a neonate is known to be a painful procedure that can result in multiple attempts 
leading to iatrogenic airway trauma. 
 
B. Physiologic responses to pain include cardiorespiratory changes (increased heart rate, increased 
respiratory rate, increased blood pressure, decreased oxygen saturation) and hormonal changes 
(specifically increases in cortisol, catecholamines, growth hormone, glucagon, and insulin levels). 
 
C. Additional physiologic responses occur specifically with intubation. 
 
1. Hypoxemia secondary to apnea during intubation or airway obstruction from positioning. 
 
2. Bradycardia due to vagal stimulation. 
 
3. Elevated intracranial pressure related to tachycardia and increased cerebral blood flow. 
 
D. Failed intubations are often secondary to suboptimal preparations for intubation. Optimal conditions include 
those in which the patient has minimal physiologic response to intubation, a relaxed jaw, open and 
immobile vocal cords, and suppression of the pharyngeal and laryngeal reflexes typically associated with 
intubation. 
 
E. Premedication for intubation of neonates optimizes intubation conditions, leading to a decreased number of 
attempts, decreased time for intubation, reduced airway trauma, and decreased pain.  Premedication has also 
been shown to minimize the cardiorespiratory and hormonal changes associated with awake intubation.  
 
F. Though fewer than 50% of NICUs in the United States report utilizing a standard premedication strategy, a 
2009 survey of NICUs in the United Kingdom showed that 90% of those units routinely administer 
premedication before non-emergent intubations. 
 
II. Ideal Premedication Strategy.  As proposed in the AAP recommendations, an ideal premedication strategy 
includes the following: 
 
A. Elimination of pain and discomfort. 
 
B. Elimination of physiologic abnormalities. 
 
C. Minimized time required to intubate. 
 
D. Decreased chances of traumatic injury. 
 
E. No adverse effects from premedication. 
 
III. Clinical Implications 
 
A. Premedication should be used in ALL intubations in newborns EXCEPT emergent intubation during 
resuscitations (both in and out of the delivery room) 
 
B. Individuals performing intubation should be experienced with bag/mask ventilation, knowledgeable about the

C. The ideal premedication agents are those with rapid onset, short duration, without adverse effects, and with

F. Premedication is CONTRAINDICATED in the following circumstances: 
 
1. In the delivery room. 
 
2. During an emergent resuscitation or acute deterioration. 
 
3. In infants with upper airway anomalies such as Pierre Robin sequence. This is because intubation in 
severely abnormal airways can be difficult, and the infant’s own respiratory effort may be necessary 
to maintain an open airway. 
 
IV. Role of Various Premedication Agents 
 
A. Analgesics – reduce pain and may attenuate increases in blood pressure. 
 
B. Vagolytics – prevent vagal bradycardia. 
 
C. Muscle relaxants / Paralytics – decrease the time and number of attempts required to intubate; may 
also minimize the increase in intracranial pressure. 
 
D. The combination of a sedative or analgesic, vagolytic, and muscle relaxant has been show to 
maintain appropriate blood pressure, heart rate, intracranial pressure, and oxygen saturation during 
intubation. 
 
V. Analgesics 
 
A. The most commonly used analgesics in neonates are opioids such as morphine, fentanyl, and remifentanyl. 
Opioids interact at various sites within the central and peripheral nervous systems to modify the transmission 
of pain signals, leading to diminished pain perception. 
 
B. Fentanyl is the recommended first line agent for analgesia during endotracheal intubations due to its 
rapid onset of action, short duration of action, and infrequent adverse effects. 
 
C. Remifentanyl has an even more rapid onset and shorter duration than fentanyl, but its limited use in neonates 
to date prevents this from being considered a first -line analgesic for premedication. However, it has been 
shown to provide a higher probability of achieving optimal intubation conditions compared to morphine. 
 
D. Morphine is acceptable for use as an analgesic in neonates, but its longer time to onset (5-15 minutes for IV 
use) and longer duration of action make it less than ideal for use during intubation.   In fact, the lack of effect on 
physiologic disturbances during intubation may be secondary to the longer time required to allow morphine to 
become effective.

VI. Sedatives/ Hypnotics 
 
A. Sedatives provide unconsciousness or amnesia. The most commonly used sedatives – midazolam, 
thiopental, and propofol – are acceptable for use (midazolam in term infants only), but they are not 
recommended as the first line approach to premedication.  Sedatives should not be used without 
accompanying analgesia. 
 
B. Midazolam, a commonly used benzodiazepine, has a short onset of action and typically short duration of 
action.  However, it has not been shown to reduce physiologic changes during intubation.   The side effects of 
midazolam include: 
 
1. Hypotension in term and preterm infants (especially when used with fentanyl). 
 
2. Decreased cerebral blood flow in preterm infants. 
 
3. Half-life exceeding 22 hours in term and preterm infants 
 
4. Exposure to benzyl alcohol that is an additive known to increase mortality and intraventricular 
hemorrhage in preterm infants. 
 
C. Thiopental, a barbiturate, also has a rapid onset and duration of action.  There has been some evidence of 
shortened intubation times with the use of thiopental, but it is also associated with an increased risk of 
hypotension when used in combination with fentanyl and/or midazolam. 
 
D. Propofol is commonly used in adults and older children, but its use is not as well studied in neonates.   
The benefit of propofol is that spontaneous respiration is maintained and oxygenation is improved during 
intubation. However, older patients have reported moderately severe pain at propofol injection sites. 
 
VII. Muscle Relaxants / Paralytics 
 
A. Paralytic agents minimize the increase in intracranial pressure during intubation. 
 
B. These agents must always be used with analgesics or sedatives. 
 
C. Non-depolarizing   muscle relaxants, such as pancuronium, vecuronium, and rocuronium, compete with 
acetylcholine for receptors on the motor end plate, thereby blocking muscle contraction.  Given their minimal 
effects on blood pressure and heart rate, and relatively shorter duration of action, vecuronium and rocuronium 
are preferred for premedication for intubation. 
 
D. Succinylcholine, a depolarizing agent that blocks neuromuscular transmission by binding to acetylcholine 
receptors and depolarizing muscle membranes, is also acceptable for use in neonates.   However, it is 
associated with rare serious side effects in children including hyperkalemia, myoglobinemia, and cardiac 
arrhythmias. It is also a known trigger of malignant hyperthermia.   Given these risks, succinylcholine 
iscontraindicated in the presence of hyperkalemia, renal failure, neuromuscular disease, and family history 
of malignant hyperthermia.

VIII. Vagolytics 
 
A. Vagolytic agents prevent bradycardia and decrease salivary and bronchial secretions.  Though there is a 
concern that vagolytics may mask hypoxia-induced bradycardia during intubation, most bradycardia 
during intubation is known to be secondary to vagal stimulation. 
 
B. Atropine is the preferred vagolytic agent with its short onset and duration of action. 
 
C. Glycopyrrolate may also be used in term/near-term infants, though there is limited experience in 
neonates, and its pharmacokinetcis in small preterm infants is not clear. 
 
IX. Consensus Approach. Given the lack of a clear standard for premedication for intubation of neonates, the 
consensus approach for our units will be the following protocol: 
 
A. Use premedication for all infants including those who are currently receiving other narcotics and/or sedatives, 
except when contraindicated (e.g. airway anomaly, emergent airway).   Premedication takes approximately 15 
- 30 minutes once the decision is made to intubate due to time required to obtain and infuse the medications.  
If the infant is not clinically stable enough to wait then proceed with emergent intubation. 
 
B. Recommended combination of medications: atropine + fentanyl + vecuronium. 
 
1. Atropine 0.02 mg/kg x1 IV -- push (can be given at any time during premedication process).   Unlike 
in older infants/children, the minimum atropine dose of 0.1mg does not apply for this application in 
this patient population. 
 
2. Fentanyl 1 mcg/kg x1 IV – infuse on pump over 5 minutes followed by a 2 minute flush (avoid slow push 
by hand as this increases the risk of chest wall rigidity).  If chest wall rigidity occurs, it can be overcome 
by administering vecuronium. 
 
3. Vecuronium 0.1mg/kg x1 IV – push administered after fentanyl and only once entire team is assembled 
and ready to intubate (laryngoscope and ETT in hand).   Though vecuronium acts quickly, there can be 
a lag of up to 30 seconds before full relaxation is achieved.   Bag-mask ventilation should continue 
during this time. 
 
4. Atropine/Fentanyl/Vecuronium are all available through the Pyxis for rapid administration.   There is 
no longer a wait time to receive atropine from the pharmacy 
 
C. In cases where there is clinical instability or case-specific concern about use of vecuronium, fentanyl and 
atropine OR atropine alone can be used for premedication. 
 
D. Given the risk of side effects and the lack of clear evidence of benefit, sedatives (such as midazolam 
and lorazepam) have been intentionally excluded. 
 
E. Avoid the use of muscle relaxants / paralytics without analgesia.   Ensure that the patient is 
appropriately sedated prior to paralytic administration.

Exchange Transfusion 
 
Diana Ng, N.N.P. 
 
I. Indications 
 
A. Significant unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia: Bilirubin levels at which exchange transfusion is 
recommended based on gestational age, postnatal age, hypoalbuminemia and other risk factors. If untreated, 
the neuro-toxic levels of circulating bilirubin could lead to overt and life-long CNS injury. Exchange transfusion 
rapidly lowers bilirubin load while maintaining intravascular volume, improves albumin for bilirubin binding and 
lowers deleterious circulating isoimmunizing antibodies. 
 
B. Iso-immune hemolytic disease of the newborn (HDN): Severe anemia and/or hyperbilirubinemia due to 
destruction of fetal red blood cells (RBCs) by passively acquired maternal antibodies. Exchange transfusion 
replaces antibody-coated neonatal RBCs with antigen-negative RBCs and removes free maternal antibodies 
in the plasma. 
 
C. Polycythemia: Partial exchange transfusion will reduce concentration of red cell volume and hyperviscosity in 
symptomatic infants. Use remains controversial as infants may have sustained micro-vascular changes prior 
to recognition of symptoms 
 
D. Severe anemia: Chronic hypervolemia due to fetal anemia often resulting in compensatory increased 
intravascular volume; may progress to congestive heart failure. Some infants may be normovolemic but are 
easily affected by simple transfusion or volume resuscitation. 
 
E. Uncommon & late indications: congenital leukemia, extreme thrombocytosis, neonatal hemochromatosis, 
metabolic disease, renal failure and sepsis. 
 
II. Contraindications 
 
A. Lower risk alternatives would be similarly effective.  
 
B. Clinical instability to the degree that the risks outweigh the benefits. 
 
C. Contraindications to urgent central or large bore peripheral catheter placement outweighs indication for 
exchange transfusion.

technique, utilize extreme caution to avoid air embolism and/or catheter dislodgement or 
occlusion. 
 
b. Could be performed by 1 provider. 
 
c. Anticipated time for completion: 90-120 minutes.
